Several recent papers address the use of Model-Driven Engineering (MDE) and model checking to formally represent and analyze the evolution of software artifacts, focusing on traceability, provenance, consistency, and the correspondence between models and implementations:

The paper "Model-Driven Discipline for Multi-Agent LLMs: Requirement-to-Verification Generation of Traceable System Models" (2026) proposes an MDE approach with automated traceability and formal verification using CSP model checking, supporting change-impact analysis and executable traceability across heterogeneous models.
"Towards Continuous Assurance with Formal Verification and Assurance Cases" (2025) describes a model-driven assurance pipeline that automatically regenerates traceable assurance arguments as specifications and verification results evolve, integrating formal verification and traceability.
"Towards a Model-Based Framework for Automated Traceable Systems and Probabilistic Model Checking" (2025) integrates MDE with probabilistic model checking, automating verification of evolving requirements via bidirectional traceability links between requirements and behavioral models.
"Model-based Security Engineering for Evolving Systems" (2010) uses UMLsec model verification with traceability links to implementation, updating mappings during refactoring to support evolving software.
Other relevant works in the results address consistency management, model repair, and bounded verification in evolving model-driven systems. These collectively cover formal representations of state and transitions, traceability, and the challenges of maintaining correspondence between models and implementations as systems evolve.

Recent research addresses the challenge of traceability and provenance in self-evolving AI systems, particularly regarding debugging, maintenance, and accountability. Key findings include:

Several papers propose technical solutions for versioning and provenance in evolving AI software, such as MLOps frameworks, branching development graphs, and version-aware infrastructures. These approaches aim to ensure that every modification—whether generated automatically or by developers—is recorded with its rationale, dependencies, and validation results.

Governance and accountability are addressed through cryptographic techniques, immutable logs, and co-versioning patterns. These methods help trace the propagation of changes, identify affected components, and support selective repair or rollback when errors are discovered.

Surveys and empirical studies highlight the importance of execution provenance and evidence tracing, especially in large language model (LLM) agents and agentic software engineering. These works emphasize the need for transparent, auditable records to establish accountability and facilitate debugging.

The literature also discusses the risks of uncontrolled accumulation of changes in self-modifying systems, underscoring the necessity of treating every evolutionary change as a versioned, traceable software modification—similar to best practices in traditional software engineering, but adapted for autonomous and AI-driven environments.
